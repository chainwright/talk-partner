# Day 2 sources note: what the spec's §2.6–2.13 rules rest on (checked 11 Nov 2026)

Why this note: REBUILD_SPEC §2.6–2.13 encode rules from our aphasia knowledge base. Before the write-up claims
"based on Supported Conversation for Adults with Aphasia (SCA)", each rule should point at something we actually read.
This lists what was verified on the page, what was seen only in search snippets, and what we could not reach.

## Verified on the primary page (read in full)

### Aphasia Institute, "Tips for Communicating with a Person with Aphasia" (SCA™ tip sheet, PDF)
URL: https://aphasia-institute.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/2011/01/Tips-for-Communicating-with-a-Person-with-Aphasia.pdf
Read all three pages on 11 Nov 2026. It is organised as "Get the message in / Get the message out / Verify",
"Based on the SCA™ approach". Rules it backs, with its own words:

| §2.3 one question at a time | "Communicate one idea at a time." · "Ask one thing at a time." |
| §2.6 choices over yes/no | "Multiple choice formats are better than yes/no questions, when you have a list of potential answers" |
| §2.4 yes/no with a way to answer | "Ask Yes/No questions and make sure the person has a way to respond. Use a written YES / NO if needed." |
| §2.4 yes/no general → specific | "Phrase Yes/No questions in a logical sequence (general to specific)" |

Added 11 Nov 2026 (late), for the repair card: the same sheet, text extracted from the PDF again and read in full. Its
"Getting the message out" section says, in order: "Establish a clear general topic first; and then move on to
understanding the details." · "Ask Yes/No questions and make sure the person has a way to respond. Use a written YES / NO
if needed." · "Ask one thing at a time." · "Encourage the person with aphasia to write down a word or draw if they can." ·
"Ask the person to give clues by gesturing, or pointing to objects, pictures and written key words." · "Give the person
time to respond." And on page 1: "If you do not have the time to communicate, explain this, and give a time when you will
return to finish your conversation. Make sure you do return." Rules these back:
| Repair tile | The sheet says |
|---|---|
| show me | "give clues by gesturing, or pointing to objects, pictures and written key words" |
| write it · draw | "Encourage the person with aphasia to write down a word or draw if they can" |

Not on the sheet: any "don't test / don't quiz" wording (§2.10). The sheet's "Verify" section is about checking what the
person meant, not about quizzing them. So §2.10 ("tell, don't test") is our inference from "Acknowledge that the person
with aphasia knows what they want to say but cannot say it" plus the competence framing, not a quoted rule. The write-up
must not attribute "tell, don't test" to the Aphasia Institute.

### PrAACtical AAC, "Aphasia, Supported Communication, Written Choice Strategy, + Variations" (22 Jun 2013)
URL: https://praacticalaac.org/strategy/aphasia-supported-communication-written-choice-strategy-variations/
Fetched 11 Nov 2026 (summary of the page, not every line). Backs §2.6 and the escape: the partner writes "possible
answers to conversational questions in list form", the strategy is "for communicators at the partner dependent level",
the partner "generating written key-word choices that relate to the conversational topic", and a standing choice such as
"none of the choices are appropriate, offer some others". It warns against asking yes/no questions "except perhaps 1 or 2".
It cites Lasker & Garrett (2008, ASHA Leader) and Lasker, Hux, Garrett, Moncrief & Eischeid (1997, AAC 13, 108–116).
The page gives **no fixed number of choices**; our "2 at more support, 3 at mild" (§2.1) comes from the KB and the
held-out runs, not from this source.

## Verified through an open index (abstract-level only)

### Kagan, Black, Duchan, Simmons-Mackie & Square (2001), JSLHR 44(3), 624–638, DOI 10.1044/1092-4388(2001/051)
Europe PMC record read on 11 Nov 2026 (the publisher page returned 403). Title: "Training volunteers as conversation
partners using 'Supported Conversation for Adults with Aphasia' (SCA): a controlled trial." Design: 20 trained volunteers
versus 20 untrained controls; trained volunteers rated significantly higher at acknowledging and revealing their partners'
competence; the participants with aphasia (who were not trained) also gained in social and message exchange skills.
Backs the claim in the README that SCA is an evaluated method and that **training the partner** changes the exchange.

### Simmons-Mackie, Raymer & Cherney (2016), Arch Phys Med Rehabil 97(12), 2202–2221.e8, DOI 10.1016/j.apmr.2016.03.023
Europe PMC record read on 11 Nov 2026. "Communication Partner Training in Aphasia: An Updated Systematic Review."
Quoted from the abstract: "All 25 of the current review articles reported positive changes from partner training",
"56 studies across 2 systematic reviews have reported positive outcomes", "communication partner training should be
conducted to improve partner skill". The authors ask for more high-quality trials, especially for acute aphasia.
This is the GATE's "evidence-based for chronic aphasia" citation; the record confirms it.

### de Vries, Smaling, van der Steen & Achterberg (2024), BMC Neurology 24, 319, DOI 10.1186/s12883-024-03824-8
Europe PMC record read on 11 Nov 2026 (the publisher page redirected to a login). The PAIC15 observational pain scale in
71 persons with aphasia: fair positive correlations with the self-report scales the participants could complete,
acceptable internal consistency; "Recognition of pain in persons aphasia using the PAIC15 showed mixed yet promising results."
What it means for §2.11: self-report pain scales are hard for people with aphasia and no instrument is settled. Our
"a little / some / a lot" bars are a conversation aid, **not a pain assessment**; the safety line stays, and the write-up
must not call the pain card a scale with validity.

## Seen only in search snippets (not verified on the page; do not quote in the write-up until read)
- Lasker, Hux, Garrett, Moncrief & Eischeid (1997), AAC 13, 108–116: alternating-treatment study of the Written Choice
  strategy (auditory only / visual only / standard written choice; Norman Rockwell pictures); "different accuracy
  patterns for each subject". Snippet source: https://scilit.com/publications/6c45460009b43a2b3d0b843d4b19a1e2 (403 on fetch).
- Rose, Worrall, Hickson & Hoffmann (2011), IJSLP: "Aphasia friendly written health information: content and design
  characteristics" (PubMed 21682542): participants ranked the aphasia-friendly versions as most liked. A separate
  secondary report says the recommended features are short sentences, simple vocabulary, a sans-serif font and clear
  images, and that these authors found line drawings and photographs had no significant effect on comprehension of
  written paragraphs (so pictures help preference more than comprehension). Sources seen: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21682542/,
  https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/606883/1/Short%20report%20with%20revisions%20final%20version.pdf. **Not read.**
- "Don't test" wording: no aphasia source with that phrase was found. A speech-therapy site lists "don't ask test
  questions" under **dementia** tips only (https://theadultspeechtherapyworkbook.com/?p=6424, snippet). §2.10 therefore
  stays our own rule, explained on screen in plain words, and the write-up should not cite it as an SCA rule.
- Pain self-report in aphasia: a Leiden thesis and a systematic review (snippets) say most stroke patients with aphasia
  cannot complete self-report scales and that no feasible, valid instrument exists yet; observers preferred a scale that
  combines numbers, words and faces, and found a bare 0–10 line "vague". Sources seen:
  https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/4262784 (not read).

## What this changes
1. The write-up (§6.4) may cite the Aphasia Institute sheet for keywords, one idea at a time, multiple choice over
   yes/no, verifying and the recap line; Kagan 2001 and Simmons-Mackie 2016 for the method; nothing else until read.
2. "Tell, don't test" is ours. Keep it, but describe it as our reading of the competence principle.
3. The pain card is a conversation aid, not a scale. Don't claim measurement.
4. Open: the §2.8 "is good/bad too thin for 'How was therapy?'" question has no source either way; it stays an SLP-test
   item. The §2.11 "left means the person's own left" convention has no source found; it stays a design decision to
   test with the figure drawing (Sun 15).
